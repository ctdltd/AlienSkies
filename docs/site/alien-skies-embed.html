Origin story

An idea from 2006, finished with help from Gaia

Alien Skies started around 2006 and never got finished. The math was never the hard part. What was missing was a catalog with good distances for enough stars. ESA's Hipparcos and Gaia missions supplied that.

Each star's position and distance become a point in 3-D space. Move the viewpoint to another star and every direction and brightness is worked out again. Familiar constellations stretch and break apart, and our own Sun becomes an ordinary star in someone else's sky.

mnew = M + 5·log10(dnew) − 5A star's true brightness M and its new distance d give how bright it looks from there.

What you see

Real places, real orbits, real light

Our Sun, labelled

Find home in someone else's sky

Sol is marked with its distance and brightness. The info line tells you where to look for it and how many years ago the light you're seeing left it. When it's out of view, an arrow at the edge of the screen shows which way to turn.

Two suns

Binary stars on their real orbits

In a binary system the second star is a second sun. From a planet of α Centauri A, Toliman swings between 36 AU and 11 AU over its 80-year orbit. Circumbinary worlds like Kepler-16 b circle both stars at once, see double sunsets every few weeks, and watch the suns eclipse each other.

A living planet

Worlds that keep one face to their star

Each planet follows its real Keplerian orbit and spins on its axis. On a tidally locked world the sun stays put while the stars wheel past once per orbit, with the small wobble an eccentric orbit causes. The system's other planets wander across the sky too. In TRAPPIST-1 they come close enough to show a disc with phases.

The Milky Way

Our galaxy, from over there

From nearby stars the Milky Way looks almost exactly as it does from Earth. From distant systems it shifts by parallax. A soft tint keeps it light on phones, and the detailed style adds the bulge's star clouds, the dark Great Rift and the Magellanic Clouds.

Tour mode

Fly between the stars

Tour mode visits a random planet and a random spot on its surface at an interval you choose. It flies there: the ground falls away, the view turns toward the destination star, and every star is re-projected from the moving viewpoint, so Earth's constellations reshape as you cross the gap.

And more

Made to run for hours

Earth's constellations, distorted

Draw all 88 constellation figures and watch them stretch and break apart with distance.

Local planet time

The info overlay names the world and its star, shows your position and orbit, and gives the local solar time where you stand.

Imaginary planets

Put your own planet around any named star. Its orbit and day length are worked out from the star's brightness and colour.

Day and night

When the sun is up the sky brightens and fainter stars wash out. Switch the atmosphere off for an airless world.

Your spot on the ground

Pick latitude, longitude and the direction you face. On a locked world, 90° longitude is an endless sunset.

Multi-monitor panorama

Each extra screen continues the view to the right instead of repeating it.

Live wallpaper

On Android it is also a live wallpaper. Swiping between home screens pans across the horizon.

Burn-in safe

The info overlay drifts slowly along the bottom of the screen so nothing stays in one place.

Time at any speed

Run in real time, or speed up to watch one Earth day pass every 2.4 minutes.
